Are Gas Tankless Water Heaters Efficient? A Lynn Haven, FL Guide

Yes, on paper. Gas tankless units rate higher than gas tanks, but your household's habits decide whether the savings show up on a bill.

The efficiency numbers, with their source

Gas tankless water heaters are more efficient than gas storage tanks in lab ratings. Per an information page from a major water heater manufacturer, gas tankless models range from about 0.82 to 0.95 UEF, while standard gas tanks range from about 0.60 to 0.70. UEF stands for Uniform Energy Factor, the federal efficiency rating, and a higher number means less energy wasted.

That is one manufacturer's published figure, not an independent test and not a promise for your home. Check the yellow EnergyGuide label or the ENERGY STAR listing for the exact model you are considering.

Where the savings come from, and where they leak away

A tank heater keeps dozens of gallons hot around the clock, even when nobody is home. That wasted warmth is called standby loss. A tankless unit heats only when a tap opens, so there is nothing to hold and nothing to lose.

The catch is use. Heavy hot water demand narrows the gap, since a tank that runs often wastes less standing heat. Long pipe runs, frequent short draws, and hot water that arrives lukewarm first can all cut into the benefit too.

  • Small household, low daily hot water use: savings tend to be largest.
  • Large household with back-to-back showers: savings shrink, and one unit may struggle to keep up.
  • Frequent short draws, like handwashing: some tankless models cycle on and off and deliver less.
  • Vacation homes or irregular occupancy: no standby loss makes tankless attractive.

What efficiency does not include

A higher UEF does not cover the upfront cost. Tankless units usually cost more to buy and install than tanks, and the gas line may need upsizing to feed a larger burner. Venting is different too, and condensing models need a drain for acidic condensate.

Maintenance counts. Hard-water scale can clog the heat exchanger, so many manufacturers recommend periodic descaling. In a house with an unknown hardness, test your tap first, since the city's report does not list hardness.

Check before you buy

Ask for the installed cost, not just the unit price. Ask what venting, gas line, and condensate work the job needs. Ask the maintenance schedule in writing.

How to size up your own savings

Skip the brochure math and look at your own house. Start with how many people shower in the morning and whether two taps ever run hot at once. Then check the existing heater's label for its fuel type and rated input, and look at how far the heater sits from the kitchen and the far bathroom.

A plumber can compare a tank and tankless setup against those facts. The answer often turns on flow rate, measured in gallons per minute, rather than on the efficiency rating alone.

  1. Count peak hot water draws

    List the most taps, showers, and appliances that run hot at the same moment on a busy morning.

  2. Check the gas supply

    A tankless burner draws more gas at once than a tank burner, so the gas line and meter must handle it.

  3. Plan the vent path

    Condensing models vent through plastic pipe, while non-condensing models use stainless or special-gas vent.

  4. Ask for two quotes in writing

    One for a new tank and one for tankless, each listing venting, gas work, and permits.

  5. Compare life costs

    Add install cost, maintenance, and expected energy use. The lowest sticker price is not always the lowest total.

Replacing an old tank with tankless

If your tank is failing, the choice is a like-for-like swap or a change of type. A water heater replacement with another tank is the lower-cost path. Switching to tankless usually adds venting and gas work, so ask for both options in writing.
